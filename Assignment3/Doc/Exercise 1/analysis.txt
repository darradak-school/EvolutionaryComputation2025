#### MaxCoverage Problems (2100 - 2103) ####
For the MaxCoverage problems, although the genetic algorithm is the most sophisticated of the three and initially achieves higher fitness within the first few evaluations due to its population diversity generating multiple high-fitness individuals early, this advantage quickly dissipates as the algorithm’s progress slows, getting significantly worse scores in 2102 and 2013, while getting the second-best score in 2100 and 2101. This is likely due to hyperparameter settings being a poor fit for the problem, causing lots of exploration early allowing it to achieve high fitness values, but exploiting too slowly and thus failing to reach the same heights the other algorithms are able to before running out of budget. The complexity of the GA also causes it issues, as it has to perform many more evaluations than the others, with the GA performing 20 evaluations per generation, thus meaning it has fewer generations to find a good solution. 

The EA was the best performing algorithm in this test, achieving the highest score in all three problems, and never getting stuck in a plateau. It seemingly balanced exploration and exploitation well, and its ability to flip multiple bits at once certainly helped it escaped the plateaus that RLS got caught in. 

The RLS performs well early, but it soon gets stuck in a plateau for the majority of the time, allowing the EA to overtake it every time, and the GA twice. Due to the RLSs lack of exploration ability outside of its nearby neighbourhood, once it is stuck in a plateau it cannot get out and stagnates. However regardless of this it still performed surprisingly well for such a simple algorithm, likely because the problem landscape is composed of many small plateaus separated by single-bit improvements. 

Overall, for this problem, the algorithms that made small, incremental changes seemed to perform best due to the MaxCoverage landscape being dominated by overlapping plateaus, while the GA with its crossover mechanism seemed to harm its ability to find solutions as fast as the other algorithms, although given enough budget it would most likely find the best solution out of the three algorithms presented.


#### MaxInfluence Problems (2200 - 2203) ####
For the MaxInfluence problems, the GA once again performs the worst overall, achieving the lowest final fitness in all four tests despite showing promising early results. The GA initially benefits from population diversity, with several individuals attaining high fitness values within the first few hundred evaluations. However, this advantage quickly fades as progress slows significantly. The combination of a limited evaluation budget and disruptive crossover operations likely contribute to this poor performance. The highly interdependent nature of the MaxInfluence problem means that crossover often destroys useful combinations of influential nodes rather than reinforcing them, leading to a slower convergence rate compared to the simpler algorithms.

The EA performs better than the GA but still falls short of the RLS in all the problems. While its mutation rate allows for greater exploration of the search space than RLS, the multi-bit flips occasionally disrupt already effective node selections, reducing its efficiency in this problem. The MaxInfluence landscape is highly correlated, where small, targeted changes are typically more beneficial than broad, random ones. The EAs advantage in escaping plateaus is less relevant here, and its performance curve, while steady, remains consistently behind the RLS.

Surprisingly, the RLS achieves the best results across all MaxInfluence instances, showing that even a simple local search heuristic can outperform more complex evolutionary methods under the right conditions. By flipping one bit per iteration, RLS is able to make precise, incremental adjustments, capitalizing on the problems fine-grained dependencies between variables. This allows it to continually exploit local improvements without frequently disrupting good partial solutions, resulting in smoother and faster convergence.

Overall, the MaxInfluence problem rewards precise local optimization and penalizes disruptive exploration due to the strong interdependencies between decision variables. Algorithms that perform small, controlled changes such as RLS are best suited to this type of landscape. In contrast, algorithms relying on large-scale recombination, such as GA, struggle to preserve beneficial node configurations within the limited evaluation budget. Given sufficient runtime, the GA may eventually leverage population diversity to combine complementary influence sets, but within the limit, simpler local methods clearly dominate.


#### PackWhileTravel Problems (2300- 2302) ####
For this last set of problems, the GA seems to perform far better than in the previous set of problems. In 2300 the GA shows its typical pattern of achieving higher scores early but slowing down later, however at the end it speeds up and just barely overtakes the EA to come achieve the highest score. In 2301, it is actually the fastest to converge, however the RLS catches up in the end, and the two algorithms get roughly the same score by the end. In the last problem 2302, the GA performs very poorly, after getting off to a strong start, it slows down dramatically. 

The EA consistently performs quite well, coming in at a close second to the GA in 2300, a very similar score to the GA in 2301, and just barely overtakes the RLS in the final problem 2302 to achieve the highest score of all the algorithms. The EAs balance between exploration and exploitation seems to suit this problem well, allowing it to make incremental and moderate improvements without disrupting good solutions.

The RLS performed fairly poorly in these problems, with the exception of 2302. In 2300 and 2301, it is fairly quick to converge, however it gets stuck in a plateau and stagnates for thousands of evaluations, getting trapped in local optima, allowing the EA and GA to easily overtake it in both problems. in 2302 the RLS once again performs surprisingly well and is in the lead during most of the runtime, however the EA overtakes it by the end, leaving it as the second highest score achieved.

Overall, the PackWhileTravel problems seem to reward algorithms capable of escaping long plateaus and effectively managing non-linear interactions between items and travel cost, rather than simply exploiting local neighbourhoods. Consequently, the landscape’s complexity and instance variability lead to diverse outcomes in which the more sophisticated algorithms outperform simple local search, but only when their parameters and strategy align well with the problem’s structure.
