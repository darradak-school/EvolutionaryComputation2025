All three algorithms—RLS, (1+1) EA, and Random Search—were tested on the OneMax problem with 100,000 evaluations. Both RLS and EA consistently reached the optimal value of 100, while Random Search plateaued significantly lower. 
LS and EA benefit from their ability to preserve and improve solutions incrementally. Random Search, lacking any exploitation mechanism, fails to consistently improve and shows high variability across runs.

Random Search struggles to build fitness and shows wide performance variability.

F3: 

EA and RLS both reached the optimal value of 5050. EA showed faster convergence, while RLS took longer. Random search plateaued relatively quickly. 

F18: 

EA and RLS achieved best-so-far values around 4.3, with EA slightly outperforming RLS, albeit reaching peak fitness slower than RLS. Random Search stagnated early and showed high variability.

F23: 

Both RLS and the (1+1) Evolutionary Algorithm demonstrate strong performance, converging to a best-so-far function value of approximately 10. The RLS algorithm shows slightly faster convergence than EA. Random search makes no meaningful progress in this problem.
Both EA and RLS are able to exploit this problem structure effectively through iterative improvement, while Random Search lacks the feedback mechanism to build on partial solutions. The (1+1) EA benefits from its probabilistic bit-flip strategy, allowing it to escape local optima and explore more diverse configurations.

F24: 

RLS clearly outperforms both the (1+1) Evolutionary Algorithm and Random Search, reaching a similar fitness to EA but at a faster rate. 
While EA eventually reaches a competitive fitness level, its convergence is slower than RLS. This suggests that its broader mutation strategy may be less efficient in tightly structured problems where small, precise changes are more beneficial.

F25: 

F25 is a rugged, epistatic optimization problem where each bit’s contribution to the fitness depends on its interaction with others. In this landscape, the (1+1) Evolutionary Algorithm (EA) — represented by the green solid line — performs best, converging to a best-so-far value close to –0.3. RLS (red dashed line) follows closely, reaching a similar final value but with slightly slower convergence. Random Search (black dashed line) performs worst. 
EA’s probabilistic mutation allows it to flip multiple bits occasionally, helping it escape local optima and explore more diverse regions of the search space. This gives it a clear edge in convergence speed and final performance.
Despite its single-bit mutation strategy, RLS steadily improves and reaches a comparable final fitness. However, its slower convergence suggests that it struggles more with the deceptive nature of the problem landscape.
Without any exploitation mechanism, Random Search fails to adapt to the problem’s complexity.

summary:

On unimodal functions (F1–F3), both RLS and the (1+1) EA demonstrate clear advantages through rapid 
and consistent convergence, outperforming Random Search by effectively exploiting the fitness gradient. 
On more complex, epistatic landscapes (F18, F23–F25), performance differences become more nuanced. 
While Random Search consistently underperforms due to its lack of exploitation, EA and RLS show varying 
strengths depending on the problem structure. EA’s probabilistic mutation enables broader exploration, 
giving it an edge on rugged landscapes like F25, whereas RLS excels in tightly structured problems such as F24
 due to its precise, single-bit mutation.
