Let X be the number of times the optimal bitstring (all ones) is sampled during T = 2^n/2 evaluations of Random Search.

Each sample is independant and uniform over {0, 1}^N
probability of hitting the optimum in one trial is: 

p = 1 / 2^n

We want to bound the probability that at least one hit occurs
Using chernoff bounds, we get:

Pr[X = 0] ≤ e^(−μ) = e^(−2^(−n/2))

Therefore, the probability of success is:

Pr[X ≥ 1] = 1 − Pr[X = 0] ≥ 1 − e^(−2^(−n/2)).

Since 2^(−n/2) is exponentially small in n, we conclude:

Pr[success] ≤ 2^(−n/2) = e^(−Ω(n)),

hence

Pr[failure] ≥ 1 − e^(−Ω(n))

Random Search requires at least 2^(n/2) fitness evaluations to reach the optimum of the F1 function with probability at least 1 − e^(−Ω(n)). 
This shows that Random Search is exponentially unlikely to succeed within a sub-exponential budget.


PROOF 2

RLS flips exactly one bit per iteration and accepts non-worsening moves. At each step, the probability of flipping a 0 to 1 is proportional to the number of 
0s remaining. The expected time to flip each 0 to 1 is n/k, where k is the number of remaining 0s. Summing over all k from 1 to n gives a total expected runtime of
O(n log n). Since RLS always accepts improvements and never regresses, the probability of reaching the optimum in this time is bounded below by a constant, i.e., Ω(1).

The (1+1) EA flips each bit independently with probability 1/n. The probability of flipping exactly one 0 to 1 and no other bits is 
approximately (n−k)/n · (1−1/n)ⁿ⁻¹ ≈ (n−k)/(n·e), where k is the number of 1s in the current bitstring. The expected time to increase the number of 1s by one
is O(n/(n−k)), and summing over all k yields an expected runtime of O(n log n). Like RLS, the (1+1) EA uses elitist selection and avoids regressions, so the 
probability of achieving this runtime is also Ω(1).

both algorithms reach the optimal search point for F3 in O(n log n) fitness evaluations with probability Ω(1).
